# Proposal

## Use when

The reader decides whether the work happens at all: a client weighing an
engagement, a sponsor approving budget, a team choosing a direction. The
document argues for one course of action and ends with a clear ask.

## Do not use when

The decision is already made; that is an implementation plan or a spec.
The document reports on finished work; that is a report or project recap.
The reader asked for a neutral comparison of options with no
recommendation; a proposal always recommends.

## Structure

1. **Header:** title, one-line subtitle naming what is proposed, labeled
   metadata (prepared for, prepared by, date, valid until when relevant).
2. **The situation:** the problem or opportunity in the reader's terms,
   and the cost of doing nothing. Short; the reader usually knows, and the
   section proves the author does too.
3. **The proposal:** what will be done and what the reader gets, stated up
   front, before any justification. A reader who stops here knows what is
   on the table.
4. **How it would work:** the approach in enough detail to be credible,
   not enough to be a spec. Phases or workstreams with what each delivers.
5. **Options considered** (when real alternatives exist): the paths not
   recommended and the one-line reason each lost. Builds trust; never pad
   with straw men.
6. **Effort and cost:** numbers the reader budgets against, in a table
   when there are tiers or phases. Only real numbers; a price that is
   still open is an open item, not a placeholder.
7. **The ask:** the single section the document exists for. What decision
   is requested, from whom, by when, and what happens right after a yes.

## Creation guidance

The register is confident and concrete, never salesy. Claims that can carry
a number carry one; claims that cannot are cut rather than inflated. If the
source includes a deadline or an expiry for the offer, it goes in the
metadata and the ask, not buried in prose.

## Hierarchy contract

The proposal statement (section 3) is the strongest block after the title.
The ask is visually unmissable at the end and echoed in the lead. Cost
tables are calm and precise; nothing on the page shouts. Options considered
reads quieter than the recommended path.

## Mobile contract

First two phone screens: title, metadata, the situation, and the proposal
statement. The cost table keeps each line item's label and amount on one
line; the ask is never below anything skippable.

## Review contract

Stable ids per SKILL.md § Review-ready contract. The ask and each cost line
are individually anchorable; when a client counter-proposes, their comment
should attach to the exact number or term. Revisions after negotiation keep
section ids so the comment history reads as one negotiation thread.

## Failure modes

**Buried ask.** The reader finishes and does not know what they are being
asked to approve. The ask is a section, named, at the end, echoed up top.

**Spec creep.** Ten pages of how before what. Detail beyond credibility
belongs in the spec that follows a yes.

**Straw-man options.** Alternatives listed only to look considered. If no
real alternative existed, drop the section.

**Placeholder numbers.** "TBD" in a cost table, or a suspiciously round
number with no basis. An unpriced item is listed as open, with what is
needed to price it.

**Hype register.** Superlatives and adjectives where evidence should be.
One concrete proof (a prior result, a relevant constraint handled) beats
any amount of enthusiasm.

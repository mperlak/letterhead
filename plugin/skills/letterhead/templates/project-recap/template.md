# Project recap

## Use when

A project, phase, or engagement is ending and the reader needs the record:
a client receiving the handoff, a sponsor closing the budget line, a team
archiving what it learned. This is the document people find a year later;
write it for that reader too.

## Do not use when

The work continues next week; that is a status update. The reader must
approve more work that follows; that is a proposal (which may cite this
recap). The document assesses quality against criteria; that is an audit.

## Structure

1. **Header:** title, one-line subtitle naming the project and its span,
   labeled metadata (client or sponsor, team, period, status: closed or
   closing).
2. **What was delivered:** the outcomes, concrete and verifiable, up
   front. Links or names of the delivered artifacts where they exist.
3. **Against the plan:** what was promised versus what shipped, including
   scope that moved and why. Honest divergence stated plainly builds more
   trust than a smoothed story.
4. **How it went:** the shape of the work: what took longer, what
   surprised, key decisions made along the way and their reasoning. Short
   narrative, not a diary.
5. **What we learned:** the two to five things worth carrying forward,
   each stated so the next project can act on it. Learnings are conclusions,
   not complaints.
6. **Open items and handoff:** what remains, who owns each item now, and
   where the living materials are (repos, credentials handed over, docs).
   After this section the authors are no longer the owners; the section
   must be complete enough for that.

## Creation guidance

The recap's numbers come from the project's own record (the plan, the
status updates, the tracker); never reconstruct from memory when a source
exists. When earlier letterhead documents exist for this project (plan,
updates), mirror their vocabulary so the series reads as one story.

## Hierarchy contract

Delivered outcomes are the strongest block after the title. Against-the-plan
reads as a calm two-column comparison or labeled list, not a confession or
a victory lap. Open items and handoff are typeset as carefully as the
opening; they are the part the future reader acts on.

## Mobile contract

First two phone screens: title, metadata, delivered outcomes complete.
Comparison structures stack cleanly with promised and shipped kept adjacent
per item; the handoff list keeps item and owner on one line.

## Review contract

Stable ids per SKILL.md § Review-ready contract. Each open item is its own
anchor point; sign-off style comments ("confirmed received") land on exact
items. A recap usually has one review round; ids still matter because the
corrected version must not orphan the sign-offs.

## Failure modes

**Victory-lap register.** Only wins, no divergence. The reader has the
plan; a recap that contradicts it silently costs the relationship more
than any slipped milestone did.

**Diary structure.** Week-by-week retelling. The reader wants outcomes,
divergence, learnings; time order is the least useful order.

**Blame in learnings.** "The client was slow with decisions" is a
complaint. "Decision latency cost ~2 weeks; next engagement gets a named
decision-owner and an SLA in the contract" is a learning.

**Orphan handoff.** An open item with no owner is not handed off; it is
dropped with paperwork. Every item gets a name.

**Missing artifact trail.** "The new system is live" with no link, repo,
or location. The year-later reader must be able to find every deliverable
from this document alone.
